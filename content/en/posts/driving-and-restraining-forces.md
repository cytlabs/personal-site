I used to wonder why people did not change things they knew were wrong, or adopt an approach they knew was better.

Over time, I have come to think that knowing what is right is not enough to make something happen. We also need to examine the forces pushing it forward and those holding it back.

I think of this as a simple model of driving and restraining forces: change becomes more likely when the forces pushing for it outweigh those maintaining the status quo.

This is not a precise equation. It is a perspective we can try when examining personal decisions, business operations and even the formation of public policy.

## Knowing change is necessary does not make it happen

Everyone knows the advice to prepare for danger in times of peace. Following it is difficult.

Imagine a company with stable customers and adequate revenue. Someone proposes investing in new products, reorganizing teams and exploring new lines of business.

These may be sensible ideas, but their costs are immediate: money, learning, the risk of failure and the disruption of established relationships.

The consequences of doing nothing lie in the future. Whether competitors will catch up or customers will leave remains uncertain.

Maintaining the status quo therefore becomes an easy choice.

Only when orders shrink, profits fall and the old approach stops working does change become urgent. At that point, the cost of doing nothing has overtaken the cost of changing.

When I say that many changes are forced by circumstances, I mean that a previously tolerable situation gradually becomes intolerable.

## Pressure prompts action, but not necessarily progress

This does not mean that pressure inevitably improves things.

A company facing competition might improve its products and efficiency. It might also cut employee compensation, reduce quality or try to prevent competitors from entering the market.

A person in difficulty might learn, seek help or try a different path. They might also avoid the problem or give up.

Pressure creates a need to respond. The response depends on the available options and the costs attached to each one.

Events therefore do not always move in the direction we consider right. They may follow an easier path even when it creates greater long-term problems.

We need to ask which response is easiest for the actual decision-maker, and which consequences they find hardest to bear.

## Choices depend on the position a person occupies

It is easy to explain behavior through character: someone lacks awareness, responsibility or foresight.

These factors exist. But another question deserves attention: why do different people placed in the same position often make similar choices?

A manager may know that a reform will benefit the company over the long term, while bearing its risks personally and waiting years for results. Preserving current performance can feel safer.

An employee may know that a process is flawed, but pointing it out creates extra work or offends colleagues. Staying silent carries no immediate loss.

From the outside, these may look like poor collective choices. From each person's position, their behavior has understandable reasons.

Rules and incentives help explain this. They reward some behaviors while making others costly.

[Douglass North's work on institutions and incentives](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1993/north/lecture/) offers this perspective: social rules and constraints shape people's choices and, through them, the direction of development.

To change an organization, asking everyone to become more conscientious is often insufficient. We also need to examine what its existing arrangements encourage.

## Many outcomes were never fully designed by anyone

This perspective also helps explain strategic interaction.

People involved in the same situation have different goals, resources and concerns. Some push forward, some resist, some wait, and others hope someone else will bear the initial cost.

The eventual outcome may be what no participant originally wanted. It emerges from their influence on one another, compromises and adjustments.

[James Buchanan's public choice theory](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1986/buchanan/lecture/) brings the analysis of individual choice and interests into politics. People making public decisions are also influenced by their own interests and the rules under which they operate.

This does not mean everyone is motivated only by selfishness. Responsibility, conviction and goodwill can also drive action. Understanding reality requires considering these alongside other interests, costs and constraints.

Policy, business and personal behavior share something at this level: actual people make choices, and those choices influence one another.

## People cannot see every option clearly

The model also needs to acknowledge that people are not all-knowing.

We may struggle to assess even our own driving and restraining forces. Immediate difficulties are concrete while future rewards are vague. Familiar paths feel safe, and the risks of unfamiliar ones may be exaggerated.

[Herbert Simon's bounded rationality](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/simon-lecture.pdf) emphasizes the limits of information, time and cognitive capacity. People often find a satisfactory solution rather than exhaust every possibility and identify an optimal answer.

People therefore tend toward what seems more acceptable within their own understanding. That understanding can be mistaken and can change with experience and new information.

This helps explain why one person sees opportunity where another sees a threat.

## Where should we direct our attention?

If motivations, constraints and strategic interaction help explain so much, should we try to study everything?

I do not think we need to.

Our time and energy are limited. The problems we encounter vastly outnumber those we can understand carefully or participate in meaningfully.

Following policy and reading history can help us understand our environment and recurring patterns. How much attention we devote to them still needs to relate to our own circumstances.

How will this affect my life? Which decisions would understanding it change? Am I willing to act on it?

To me, attending to the responsibilities of one's position means allocating attention according to the responsibilities we currently carry, while leaving room to learn about the places we hope to reach.

Curiosity, concern for others and understanding the world have value in themselves. But widespread discussion of an issue does not oblige us to follow it continuously, state an opinion or carry every emotion surrounding it.

## Making an explanation useful

Saying that something happened because driving forces outweighed restraining forces can seem to explain almost anything. Used only after the event, it can become an untestable statement.

It is more useful to identify the specifics beforehand.

Who wants it to happen? Who does not? Who pays the cost of change? Who bears the consequences of the status quo? What changes in conditions could alter their choices?

These questions can turn a vague judgment into something clearer.

The same applies personally. When something has not started, we can ask whether the first step is too difficult, the reward too distant or the cost of failure too high, and whether one obstacle can be reduced.

[Kurt Lewin's force-field analysis](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/001872674700100103) is close to this approach: a situation can be understood as an equilibrium between driving and restraining forces, with change involving shifts in their relationship.

Sometimes removing one concrete obstacle is what allows things to move forward. Understanding human limitations and how environments shape choices can also help us address our own problems more concretely.
