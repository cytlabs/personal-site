Lately, while building products, I've kept running into the same situation: I turn to AI to save time, then end up spending a lot of time redoing the work.

With AI available, it's easy for me to want to hand everything over to it. I let it keep going before I've fully thought through the requirements. Once it has generated a plan or a document, I put off reading parts that deserve a careful look.

It's only when I see the result that I realize how much of it differs from what I wanted. Then comes round after round of revisions. Sometimes I even get angry: why can't it produce what I want, no matter how I explain it?

Later, I realized that sometimes I hadn't worked out what I wanted either.

I had postponed the thinking that should have happened earlier until after I saw the result. The unresolved questions still had to be faced, but by then they affected more of the work and were harder to fix.

That made me start asking myself again: what exactly am I hoping AI will save me? And where do I still need to be involved?

## I keep wanting to turn problems into Skills

I've noticed another habit of mine: I keep wanting to turn everything into a Skill.

When I run into a problem, my first thought is often whether a tool could solve it. Could I turn it into a Skill? Could I connect a few tools and have AI do it automatically?

I still see value in all of this. Methods that already work can become reusable, repetitive tasks can be reduced, and tools can work better together.

But now I remind myself that if a method hasn't been tested, writing it into a Skill doesn't prove that it's right.

A workflow can look complete and a workbench can look polished, while the real problem remains unsolved. What does the customer actually need? What information is still unclear? What would count as an acceptable result? The answers need to be grounded in the actual work.

Building tools makes it easy to feel a clear sense of progress. Improving a Skill, connecting a service and organizing a workflow all leave visible results.

Understanding a vague requirement, dealing with disappointing feedback or admitting that the original idea might be flawed is less comfortable.

So I've started watching out for a particular situation: putting a lot of effort into building tools to solve a problem, while taking too long to return to the problem itself.

## The output is there, but understanding may not have caught up

When I wrote plans, mapped out workflows and built products myself, much of the thinking happened through doing the work.

If I got stuck on a section, it often meant there was still something I hadn't thought through. If two steps didn't fit together, I had to go back and understand how they related. The process was slow, but it also helped me form my own judgment.

Now AI can quickly produce a complete document, or even build the product. The output is already there, but our understanding of the work may not have caught up.

A document identifying the users doesn't mean we've verified what those users actually need. A plan containing a workflow doesn't mean the people involved can follow it in practice.

This is something I'm paying more attention to: there are documents I still need to read carefully, even when AI has written them. There are choices I still need to understand, even when AI has already made a recommendation. Why did it choose that option, and does it fit the situation in front of me?

Of course, rework isn't always caused by people failing to think things through. AI can misunderstand things, miss requirements or simply do a poor job. Some requirements can only become clear gradually, after we see and try a prototype.

I want to cut down on the times when I invest heavily in implementation before key questions are settled, only to discover later that the direction is off. Could a small prototype or a trial in actual use reveal the problems first? This is another way of working together that people and AI need to explore.

## A tool's value needs to be judged in real work

Lately, I've seen many people selling Skills online, as well as many selling workbenches. I've become more cautious about these offerings.

I believe they can be useful, but how much they help depends on the task, the person using them and the actual working environment.

When a method works for someone else, I still need to check whether its assumptions hold in my work. Do I have the information they have? Is their workflow the same as mine? Do the criteria it relies on apply in my situation?

Buying a set of tools still leaves these conditions to be addressed one by one.

And "it varies from person to person" can't become an explanation for every failure. We can't give the tool all the credit when it works and put all the blame on the user when it doesn't. What a tool improves, and the situations it suits, should be testable through real tasks.

The same applies to companies.

If business processes are still unclear, departments understand the goals differently, and nobody is clear about who signs off on the results or who can decide on changes when things go off track, those questions still need to be addressed when AI is introduced.

Systems can help document workflows, pass on information and carry out tasks. The people involved still need to reach agreement and adjust their approach based on the actual results.

Individuals need to learn how to delegate, and companies need to rethink how they divide work and collaborate. Once a tool becomes part of the work, the way people work around it needs to change too.

## From hoping for replacement to reconsidering participation

I previously wrote an article called [When AI no longer needs human work, power becomes the real question](https://www.xiamu.cc/en/blog/when-ai-no-longer-needs-human-work/).

That article explored a possibility: if AI gradually becomes able to identify problems, make decisions, run businesses and even organize the production of physical goods, human labor may no longer be necessary for production systems to function. At that point, what should human dignity, access to resources and freedom of choice rest on?

I still think that question is worth discussing. My recent experience has also made me think more concretely about human participation.

I used to see handing more and more work to AI as a goal worth continuing to pursue. Now I'm more interested in asking: after handing it over, do I still understand what I'm doing? Do I know how to judge the results? Do I still have the ability to change direction?

I don't yet know how far future AGI, or even brain interfaces, might move this boundary. The fact that today's tools have limitations doesn't prove that future systems will never be able to carry out certain tasks on their own.

But even if technology becomes capable of doing more, we can still ask which processes we want to take part in and which choices we want to keep for ourselves.

If the purpose of writing is simply to get a publishable article, AI can take on much of the work. If writing also helps me work out what I actually believe, then reading, hesitating, challenging ideas and revising my views have value in themselves.

I want the freedom to choose to take part, as well as the freedom to delegate with confidence. A person's worth doesn't have to be proven by always being more useful than a machine. That is consistent with what I wrote in the earlier article.

## Reconsidering my own role and how to work with AI

When I talk about coming back to ourselves, that includes understanding ourselves better, as well as reconsidering the processes that a tool alone cannot complete.

Some difficulties come from repetitive work and can be reduced with tools. Some come from a lack of information and require investigation and experimentation. Others come from conflicting goals and require us to make trade-offs, accepting that we can't have everything for now.

AI can take part in these processes. But producing an analysis does not necessarily mean we have established the facts, reached agreement or made the choices.

I still want AI to take more tedious work off my hands, and I will keep using Skills, workbenches and other tools. At the same time, I want to be able to tell whether the problem in front of me calls for a better tool, a deeper understanding, a real conversation or a decision that still hasn't been made.

For work that has already been validated and whose results are easy to check, we can gradually hand over more. For work with changing goals, or work we're unfamiliar with, we can stay more involved and explore it together with AI.

Where my understanding, choices and actual involvement are needed, I want to be there.

I'm still trying to find that balance. I hope that using AI will leave me with a clearer understanding of the work and a greater ability to decide what to do next. How much to hand over can change as the task and my experience develop.
