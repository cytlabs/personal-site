Lately, I've found myself feeling more and more that the world is one giant makeshift operation.

I follow AI fairly closely and often see highly capable people using it exceptionally well. After seeing enough of that, it's easy to assume these things must already be commonplace.

But through some recent interactions, I realized that things I take for granted are still unfamiliar to others. Our understanding of the same technology can be very different.

It's similar in companies. Everyone is talking about putting AI to work, and some businesses have spent a lot of money, yet the results have been a complete mess. It reminds me of digital transformation projects I encountered before: they bought software, developed systems and committed staff, yet the work didn't necessarily get any better in the end.

At first, I found it hard to believe. With so many tools, methods and successful examples already available, how could so many things still be done so poorly?

Thinking about it later, though, I realized there really is a long distance between how far technology has advanced and what an individual or a company can actually achieve.

I felt these differences back when I worked in IT operations. Some companies built many of their own internal systems to automate deployment, monitoring and all kinds of repetitive tasks. They had people dedicated to building them and people dedicated to maintaining them.

At smaller and smaller companies, these systems became increasingly basic, or were absent altogether. Much of the work relied on manual effort, one person's experience or a few people working things out on the spot.

There were certainly differences in capability, but there were also very practical reasons: not enough people, not enough budget and no spare capacity to maintain an entire set of systems over the long term.

Small companies also had their advantages. A few people could discuss something and finish it in an hour or two; at a large company, getting through the process for the same task might take several days.

More systems and processes can solve some problems, but they also bring new coordination costs. A simpler team structure makes some things quicker, but when people leave or the business expands, problems that weren't properly addressed before can come to light.

Seen this way, it seems hard to sum it all up by saying, "Large companies are professional; small companies are makeshift operations."

Gradually, I've come to feel that I may have imagined the world as better put together than it really is.

When I see a company operating normally, it's easy to assume there must be a mature system behind it. When I see a project with a lot of money invested in it, I assume it must have sufficiently skilled professionals who have thought through everything they need to consider.

But once you actually get involved, you find that behind it all are individual people. Everyone has limits to their abilities, as well as their own tasks, pressures and priorities. Much of the work gets done with too little time, incomplete information and limited resources, with adjustments made along the way.

A system may be built, but someone still has to maintain it afterward. A tool may work, but someone still has to figure out how to make it part of everyday work. A person may understand the technology, but they still need others to be willing to cooperate.

Money and tools can solve some of the problems. People still have to do the rest.

And when I think about myself, the same is true.

In areas I know well, I can easily spot where others have done things poorly. But in unfamiliar areas, I also have to learn as I go. I don't always think things through, and I have to redo work too. It's hard for any of us to be fully prepared for everything.

Thinking about this, I begin to understand what it means to live among others with humility.

The world isn't as perfectly put together as we imagined, and we're part of it too. Some imperfections exist because the necessary conditions aren't yet in place; some are trade-offs that had to be made at the time; and some really do need someone to make a serious effort to fix them.

I still want to do things well, and I still find some practices unreasonable. But before judging a person or a company, I'm more inclined to understand their circumstances first: what they have to work with, what they're missing and what brought them to where they are today.

Perhaps this is what the real world looks like.

Having accepted this, I'm more interested in what we can still do well given the circumstances we face.
